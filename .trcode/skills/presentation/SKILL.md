# Presentations

## 1. Three questions before any slide
- **Audience**: who is in the room, what they already know, what they care about.
- **Decision**: what they must think, feel or approve when you finish. A deck without a decision is a document — write a document instead.
- **Format**: presented live (slides support you, few words) or sent to read (slides carry the argument, more words). These are different artefacts; do not build one and use it as the other.

## 2. Write the story first, in text
Draft the slide titles as full sentences that state the claim: "Churn comes from onboarding, not price". Read the titles in order — if that sequence alone does not make the argument, no amount of design will save it. Fix it here, where editing is cheap.

Default arc: **Situation → Complication → Question → Answer → Evidence → What we need**. Put the answer early for a senior audience; build up to it only when you must bring people along.

## 3. One idea per slide
The title is the idea. The body is the single piece of evidence for it. If a slide needs two titles, it is two slides. Six to ten words per bullet, at most four bullets — or better, replace the bullets with one chart, one image, or three words.

## 4. Numbers and charts
- One message per chart, and the message is the chart's title.
- Simplest form that carries it: line for trend, bar for comparison, single big number for a headline metric.
- Label directly instead of using a legend; drop gridlines, borders and 3D.
- Always state the source and the period. Round hard — "≈40%", not "39.7431%".

## 5. Visual discipline
Consistent template: same title position, same type scale (title / body / caption only), one accent colour, generous margins. Minimum 24pt body text for a room. Never paste a screenshot of a table nobody can read — crop to the row that matters, or restate it as a number.

## 6. Endings and appendix
Close with the ask and the next step, not "Thank you / Questions". Move backup detail, methodology and long tables into an appendix after the close — it is what makes a deck survive the Q&A.

## What not to do
- No agenda slide for a deck under ten slides.
- No wall of text read aloud.
- No animation that delays information.
- No slide you cannot explain in one sentence — cut it.

## Answer format
Slide-by-slide outline: number, sentence-title, what is on the slide, what you say. If asked to produce the deck itself, generate a self-contained HTML file with one section per slide (keyboard navigation, 16:9, the type/colour rules above) or Markdown slides — and say which tool it targets.
